Most coverage frames AI investing as the model race.

Who has the smartest model. Who leads the benchmarks. Who ships the best demo. Who closes the widest context gap this quarter.

That framing is too small.

The actual economic problem is distribution.

As frontier-model capability compresses, durable value does not flow mainly to the lab with the sharpest release cycle. It flows to the company that already controls the path through which intelligence reaches the user. The pipes matter more than the engine once engines begin to converge.

That is the next structural shift investors need to price.

Vol.1 argued that control over enterprise trust gets paid first. Vol.2 pushes one layer deeper: control over distribution gets paid bigger. Not faster. Not cleaner. Bigger.

The reason is simple. AI labs can build remarkable intelligence. They do not automatically own the right to distribute that intelligence at global scale. Distribution belongs to companies that already sit inside work, attention, search, documents, cloud, identity, devices, and default user behavior. Microsoft and Google already own those positions. Everyone else is either riding their infrastructure, attacking one flank, or trying to build a third pipe from scratch.

That is the frame of this report.

---

## 1. The Misdirection

Most investors still ask the wrong first question: "Which model wins?"

That question mattered in the early phase of the cycle because capability was scarce and visibly uneven. It matters less now because capability is increasingly becoming a purchased input. The market is filling with frontier providers, open-weight challengers, agent frameworks, and cloud wrappers. Intelligence is still expensive. It is no longer rare.

The better question is this:

Who owns the route through which AI becomes habitual?

That is not a model question. It is a distribution question.

And distribution is where the economics harden.

A model can be switched. A distribution habit is harder to uproot. A model can be benchmarked. A workflow default compounds quietly. A model can be licensed to many parties. A distribution layer monetizes repeatedly because it already owns the user relationship.

That is why the AI labs are not the whole story. In some cases, they are not even the main public-market story. OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI represent strategic value. But strategic value and investable value are not the same thing. The public market tends to pay more durably for the company that owns the installed channel, the embedded workflow, the contract structure, and the switching cost.

The labs supply intelligence. The distributors own monetization.

That distinction will shape where the largest multiples settle over the next decade.

## 2. Two Distribution Empires

The AI race is being run on top of two pre-existing empires.

Microsoft owns the pipes. Google owns the audience.

Those are different businesses, priced differently, with different forms of strategic power.

Microsoft's empire is built on enterprise embedment. Google's empire is built on mass attention and consumer default behavior. Both are distribution monopolies in all but name. The real AI race now runs through those incumbencies.

### Microsoft: The Enterprise Pipe

Microsoft sits inside the institutional bloodstream. It does not need to invent a new user habit to win AI distribution. It already owns the habit stack: Microsoft 365, Teams, Outlook, Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Windows, Active Directory, Azure, and a vast government and enterprise contract base.

In its FY26 Q2 results, Microsoft reported $81.3 billion of revenue, crossed $51.5 billion in Microsoft Cloud revenue, grew Azure 39 percent, and reported commercial remaining performance obligation of $625 billion. That is not just a software business. It is a cash-generating distribution machine with enormous contractual gravity.

The strongest version of the Microsoft thesis is not "Copilot is good." That is surface-level analysis. The stronger thesis is that Microsoft can inject AI into tools people already open every day and into procurement systems companies already fund every year. That makes Microsoft the cleanest owner of the enterprise pipe. It does not need to persuade the user to visit a new destination. It can meter AI inside existing work.

That is what makes the pipe so valuable. The user is already there.

There is another part the market underweights: the government and regulated-enterprise angle. Microsoft is not just embedded in productivity. It is embedded in institutional trust. That means its distribution power is not only broad. It is durable. When AI moves from experimentation into procurement, governance, audit, and compliance, Microsoft's route to monetization strengthens further because it already owns the systems where permissioning and document control live. That is not glamorous. It is paid.

### Google: The Audience Pipe

Google owns the audience. That is a different kind of empire.

Google does not dominate enterprise control the way Microsoft does. It dominates discovery, attention, mobile reach, and daily information access.

Alphabet's Q4 2025 results showed $113.8 billion of quarterly revenue, 31.6 percent operating margin, Google Cloud growth of 48 percent, Gemini App growth to more than 750 million monthly active users, more than 325 million paid subscriptions across consumer services, and YouTube annual revenue above $60 billion. Google also sits on Android, with core ecosystem services reaching billions of devices and nearby sharing features alone enabled on over 3 billion devices.

The strongest version of the Google thesis is not "Gemini is catching up." That is also too small. The stronger thesis is that Google can place AI into the front door of consumer intent: Search, Android, Gmail, Maps, Chrome, YouTube, and Workspace. Microsoft owns the work surface. Google owns the attention surface.

That matters because the cost of distribution is lower when the product sits inside an already dominant attention loop. Search does not need to teach people where to go. Android does not need to win a new device category. YouTube does not need to manufacture a new usage habit. Google can distribute intelligence by inserting it into behavior that already exists at planetary scale.

That is why Google's moat is not only model quality. It is audience control. And audience control compounds because usage creates data, data improves product relevance, relevance increases frequency, and frequency increases default power.

Microsoft owns the pipes through which institutions operate.

Google owns the audience through which people navigate the digital world.

Those are the two great distribution empires of the AI age. Everyone else is either leasing one of them, complementing one of them, or trying to build a third.

## 3. The 5-Driver Framework

To analyze distribution structurally, the Brutal Edge framework uses five drivers.

1. **Reach** - how many users you can touch
2. **Frequency** - how often they use it
3. **Stickiness** - cost of switching
4. **Data flywheel** - whether usage makes the system stronger
5. **Embed depth** - how critical the product is to core workflows

Each driver scores 1 to 5. Total possible score: 25. Convert to a Distribution Score out of 100.

This framework matters because "distribution" is too vague on its own. Investors need a way to distinguish weak reach from strong embedment, or broad reach from shallow monetization. These five drivers separate a large audience from a real moat.

### Microsoft Through the Five Drivers

**Reach: 5.** Microsoft 365 paid commercial seats now exceed 450 million by industry tracking of Microsoft's latest disclosure, and Azure gives Microsoft a second giant enterprise channel.

**Frequency: 5.** Outlook, Teams, Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Windows are not occasional products. They are daily operating surfaces.

**Stickiness: 5.** Switching cost is high because Microsoft products are tied to identity, files, institutional memory, security policy, and cross-team workflows.

**Data flywheel: 4.** Microsoft benefits from usage data across productivity and cloud, but its main strength is not open consumer data scale. It is structured workflow embedment.

**Embed depth: 5.** Microsoft is not an app. It is part of the enterprise operating environment.

That yields 24 out of 25, or 96 on a flat conversion. The Brutal Edge Distribution Score is adjusted to 94 to account for enterprise friction, procurement drag, and the possibility of partial Copilot adoption. The structural point still stands: Microsoft owns the deepest AI distribution pipe in enterprise software.

### Google Through the Five Drivers

**Reach: 5.** Search, Android, Gmail, Workspace, Chrome, and YouTube give Google one of the broadest user networks on earth. Gemini alone now has more than 750 million monthly active users. Android ecosystem services touch billions of devices.

**Frequency: 5.** Search, Gmail, YouTube, and Android are not weekly products. They are recurring habits many times per day.

**Stickiness: 4.** Consumer switching cost is lower than enterprise switching cost. But default status in search, mobile, and video creates a behavioral moat that holds even when alternatives exist.

**Data flywheel: 5.** Usage directly strengthens ranking, relevance, ad targeting, and product iteration. The loop is faster than any enterprise channel.

**Embed depth: 3.** Google's consumer products are frequent, but not always mission-critical in the same way Microsoft sits inside work. Workspace raises that score, but Search and YouTube remain shallower as workflow infrastructure.

That yields 22 out of 25, or 88. Lower embed depth than Microsoft. Stronger audience-scale data loop. Different moat. Same empire.

The key idea is this:

Microsoft's distribution is narrower but deeper. Google's distribution is broader but lighter.

Both are elite. They just get paid through different channels.

## 4. The Emerging Challengers

The AI market does not stop with two empires. It does, however, force every challenger to answer one question:

If Microsoft owns the pipes and Google owns the audience, where does a third route come from?

### Amazon: The Infrastructure Challenger

Amazon is the most credible challenger because it owns AWS.

That matters. AWS is already a foundational infrastructure layer for enterprise software, and Amazon Bedrock gives Amazon a way to distribute models and agent systems through existing cloud relationships. Amazon also has Alexa sitting inside hundreds of millions of devices.

Amazon has now deepened its Anthropic relationship materially. Recent reporting indicates Amazon has committed an additional $5 billion investment in Anthropic, building on the cloud and capacity partnership already in place. This is separate from Alphabet's reported $40 billion commitment to Anthropic disclosed in late April 2026, which goes through Google Cloud and represents a different kind of strategic alignment.

Two of the three largest hyperscalers are now anchored to a single frontier lab through different routes. That is not just a capital story. It is a distribution alignment. Amazon wants Bedrock and AWS to become the enterprise routing layer through which outside model intelligence reaches corporate users. Alphabet wants Google Cloud to do the same through a different capacity arrangement.

The signal investors should take from this is structural, not narrative. The hyperscalers are not betting on whether the labs will produce strong models. They are betting on owning the enterprise distribution layer through which those models reach paying customers.

### Meta: The Social Challenger

Meta's edge is not enterprise pipes. It is massive social frequency.

Meta reported 3.58 billion Family daily active people in Q4 2025. That is extraordinary reach and frequency. LLaMA also gives Meta a distribution strategy that differs from Microsoft's and Google's. Instead of owning the enterprise workflow or the search habit, Meta is using open-weight distribution and app-level embedment to spread its intelligence layer across social surfaces, messaging, creator tools, and consumer interfaces.

But neither Amazon nor Meta yet fully owns a third AI pipe in the same sense Microsoft and Google do.

Amazon owns infrastructure and can rent distribution into enterprise. That is powerful, but it still sits one layer below the end user. Meta owns attention inside social environments, but it does not yet control the same kind of cross-workflow or cross-intent operating surfaces.

This is why both score below the top two. They are real challengers. They are not yet distribution empires of equal weight.

### The Labs

OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI matter strategically. They do not yet own public-market distribution in the same way the incumbents do. They need clouds, apps, search positions, hardware partners, or enterprise channels. Their strategic value is high. Their direct public-market access is mostly absent. That is why investors who confuse "important company" with "best public equity expression" will keep making category errors in this sector.

These are different businesses, priced differently, with different monetization rhythms. Treating AI as a single basket misses the structural distinctions that determine where the durable returns will settle.

## 6. Investment Translation

So how does this translate into returns?

**First, the infrastructure names get paid first and fastest.** NVIDIA and the hyperscaler capex names monetize the buildout immediately because everyone needs chips, power, and cloud spend before distribution matures. That is the cleanest near-term trade and it is already in motion.

**Second, the distribution incumbents get paid biggest, but on the longest timeline.** Microsoft and Google do not have the cleanest near-term narrative every quarter. The market gets distracted by capex, model headlines, and antitrust noise. But structurally they own the routes through which enterprise trust and user habit already flow. That is the deepest moat in the whole stack. It is the slowest to price and the hardest to dislodge.

**Third, the labs have high strategic value and limited direct public access.** Investors can admire Anthropic or OpenAI without pretending they are public equities. The Alphabet-Anthropic and Amazon-Anthropic alignments are the cleanest current expressions of frontier-lab exposure available in public markets. They are still indirect. They are not the same as owning the labs.

**Fourth, Amazon and Meta offer the asymmetric third-pipe trade.** Amazon can turn Bedrock and AWS into an enterprise distribution layer if it closes the end-user gap. Meta can turn LLaMA and its social graph into a meaningful AI channel if it converts attention into deeper utility. Those are real upside cases. They are just not as structurally proven as Microsoft's and Google's distribution positions today.

The deepest investment conclusion is this:

The AI economy does not mainly reward the best model. It rewards the owner of the route through which the model becomes habitual.

That is where the largest durable multiples tend to settle.
