# THE MENTAL GAME #004
## The Anchoring Trap: Why Your First Price Becomes Your Prison

There is a kind of investor pain that does not show up in any brokerage statement.

It is the pain of watching a great business compound from outside the walls of a prison you built yourself. The walls are not made of leverage, or losses, or bad timing. They are made of a single number — the first price you saw.

This piece is about that prison. And about how to walk out of it.

---

## 1. I'm Going to Confess Something First

Let me be honest with you.

Every morning, when I open the charts, I'm fighting ghosts.

I see NVIDIA and a number flashes through my head — the price I almost bought it at, years ago, before it was obvious. I see Bitcoin and a different number appears — a level I once told myself I'd "wait for." I see Innodata, a stock we just published a full report on, and there's another ghost — the price it was at before the earnings repricing.

Those numbers don't exist anywhere on the chart anymore. The market has moved on. The businesses have moved on. The world has moved on.

But somewhere in my head, those numbers are still standing there. Still whispering. Still telling me the current price is "too high," "already missed," or "wait for the pullback."

I'm telling you this because if I'm honest about my own ghost charts, then the rest of this piece can be honest too. This is not a lecture from someone who has solved the problem. This is a frame from someone who fights it every single day — and who has found a few tools that work better than willpower.

If you have ever stared at a stock and thought, "I would have bought it at the old price, but now it's too far," this piece is for you.

It's also for me.

---

## 2. The Ghost in the Chart

Every investor has a ghost chart.

It is the chart of a stock you first studied at one price and now cannot mentally reprice at another.

You saw the company at $100.
It went to $150.
You told yourself you missed it.
It went to $300.
You stopped looking.

The price changed. Your mind did not.

That is anchoring.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described anchoring as the tendency to rely too heavily on the first number we encounter when making judgments under uncertainty. In investing, that first number is often the first price we saw, the 52-week high, the price we almost bought at, or the price we once sold at.

The problem is brutal:

That number usually has nothing to do with future value.

It is not analysis.
It is a residue of timing.

And yet investors build entire decisions around it — including, on bad days, me.

---

## 3. Why "I Missed It" Is So Destructive

Most long-term investors do not miss great stocks because the stocks are genuinely too expensive.

They miss them because the stock became expensive *relative to an old anchor*, not relative to future earnings power.

Here is how the trap works.

**Step 1: Discovery.** You find a company at $100. The story is strong, but you are not fully convinced. You wait.

**Step 2: Movement.** The stock goes to $150. You do not update your thesis. You update your regret.

**Step 3: Paralysis.** The business improves. The earnings power rises. The fair value may have gone from $140 to $500. But your brain is still comparing $150 to $100, not $150 to future value.

**Step 4: Emotional Exit.** At $300, you no longer feel disciplined. You feel excluded. So you remove the stock from your mental universe entirely.

That is the hidden cost of anchoring: it turns missed entry points into permanent blindness.

This is why some investors can correctly identify the next great company and still never make money from it. Their analysis was good enough to find the opportunity. Their psychology was too weak to reprice it.

I have watched this happen to me on more than one position. The thesis was right. The analysis was right. The framework was right. The anchor killed the trade.

---

## 4. We Are Living Through a Repricing Era, Not a "Too Far" Era

This matters even more in 2026 because some sectors are not merely going up.

They are being structurally repriced.

That distinction is everything.

A stock moving higher because of speculation is one thing.
A business being valued differently because its earnings power, industry role, and scarcity profile have changed is something else entirely.

Many investors are still using old anchors in markets that no longer fit old frameworks.

### Memory and AI Infrastructure

A large number of investors still think about memory as a classic cyclical commodity business because that is how they were trained to think about it. Their anchor is the old cycle. Their models are built around the old ceiling. Their reflex is to fade strength.

But when a market shifts from ordinary demand to infrastructure demand, old peaks may no longer be true peaks. In a repricing regime, what once looked like the top of the cycle may eventually look like the beginning of a new one.

If you are still asking, "Hasn't memory already had its run?" you may be asking last cycle's question in this cycle's market.

### AI Data Infrastructure

A company like Innodata can rise sharply after a major earnings surprise, and the reflexive response is immediate: "It moved too much."

But "too much" compared to what?

Compared to last week's price? Or compared to what the market may now be realizing about its role in the AI stack?

Those are not the same question.

Anchoring makes investors ask the lazy one.

The repricing era punishes lazy questions more than any other kind of market does.

---

## 5. Reverse Anchoring: Why Falling Stocks Look "Cheap"

Anchoring is not only a bull-market problem.

It becomes even more dangerous in falling stocks.

This is where anchoring merges with the confirmation loop we covered in The Mental Game #003.

An investor sees a stock fall from $200 to $150 and immediately thinks: *"It's cheaper now."*

Cheaper than what?

Cheaper than the old price, yes.
Cheaper than the future business value, maybe not.

That is reverse anchoring. A broken stock can look attractive simply because the mind is comparing it to a higher past price rather than to a lower future earnings path.

This is how investors get trapped in deteriorating situations. They do not re-underwrite the business. They revere the old high.

A stock that is down 25% is not necessarily on sale. It may simply be late to a much larger repricing lower. A lower price is not evidence of value. It is just a lower price.

That distinction saves portfolios.

---

## 6. The Mental Mistake Behind Cost Basis Worship

One of the worst habits in investing is treating your cost basis as if it has analytical meaning.

It does not.

Your purchase price matters for taxes. It matters for your return history. It does not matter to the business.

The company does not know where you bought it.
The market does not care where you bought it.
Future cash flows do not care where you bought it.

And yet investors constantly ask the wrong question:

*"Should I buy more because it's below my cost?"*

That is not a valuation question. It is an ego question.

A better question is:

*If I had no position today, would I want to initiate one at this price, with this business, under these conditions?*

That is the beginning of intellectual honesty.

Not historical loyalty.
Not emotional bookkeeping.
Not identity defense.

Just honest underwriting.

I have to ask myself this question on most positions, most months. Not because I always change my mind, but because the question itself prevents the slow drift from analysis into attachment.

## 7. A Better Framework for Long-Term Investors

If you want to break the anchoring trap, you need a process that forces you to think forward, not backward.

Here are three tools that matter.

### Zero-Base Thinking

Ask yourself:

*If I discovered this stock for the first time today, at this exact price, would I buy it?*

This is the cleanest antidote to anchoring because it strips out the emotional privilege of the first price you saw.

The first time I started using this, I was uncomfortable with how often the answer was *yes* on stocks I had told myself were "too far." That discomfort is the point. It tells you the prison is real.

### Anchor to Forward Earnings, Not Historical Prices

Do not anchor to the old chart. Anchor to the next two to three years of earnings power, free cash flow, margin structure, and industry position.

Past price is memory.
Forward earnings are analysis.

Memory is not free. It costs you the compounding you don't participate in.

### Interrogate the Change

If a stock moved sharply, force yourself to ask:

- Did sentiment change?
- Did the multiple change?
- Did the business change?
- Did the industry change?
- Did the market discover something it previously underpriced?

You need to know whether the move reflects emotion or repricing.

Because if it is repricing, the old anchor becomes worse than useless. It becomes misleading.

---

## 8. Why This Matters More in America's Current Market

This is especially relevant for U.S. investors right now because the most powerful parts of the market are not moving randomly.

They are moving around structural winners.

AI infrastructure, compute, memory, data engineering, power, cloud platforms, defense technology, and select industrial automation names are all being repriced based on a world that looks different from the one investors used even two years ago.

That means old mental reference points are increasingly unreliable.

If you still anchor to where Microsoft, NVIDIA, Broadcom, Alphabet, or a second-order AI enabler used to trade before the market understood the earnings leverage, you may spend the next few years "waiting for a pullback" that never gives you the old entry.

This is one of the cruelest truths in investing:

**The best businesses often feel expensive for most of the move.**

That does not mean you should chase anything that is going up. It means you must learn the difference between a stock being overextended and a stock being repriced into a new earnings reality.

That is a far more demanding skill.

But it is also a far more valuable one.

---

## 9. What Charlie Munger Understood That Most Investors Still Don't

Charlie Munger's deeper lesson was never just "buy great businesses."

It was: *stop letting cheapness seduce you into mediocrity, and stop letting past price blind you to quality.*

His real edge was not brilliance alone. It was the refusal to let old anchors dominate present thinking.

He understood that opportunity cost is not a side topic in investing. It is the topic.

If you refuse to buy a compounding machine because it is up from where you first saw it, you are not being disciplined. You are paying a hidden tax in the form of missed compounding.

That tax does not show up in your brokerage statement.

It shows up in the life your portfolio could have had.

---

## 10. The Way Out

Your first price is not a sacred reference point.

It is just a number.

The stock does not know it.
The business does not know it.
The future does not know it.

So here is the discipline:

Stop comparing the stock to where you first saw it.
Stop comparing today's price to yesterday's regret.
Stop confusing a lower old number with a better opportunity.

Instead, ask:

- What is the business worth going forward?
- What changed in the thesis?
- What changed in the earnings path?
- What changed in the strategic position?
- What changed in the market's understanding?

That is how you get out of the prison.

Not by becoming emotionless.
By becoming more honest about what your emotions are doing.

---

## Closing

The anchoring trap is so dangerous because it feels rational.

It feels disciplined to "wait for a better price."
It feels prudent to avoid buying above where you first saw the stock.
It feels safe to call a falling stock "cheap" because it used to be higher.

But those feelings are often just old numbers wearing the costume of analysis.

Your first price is not truth.
It is just memory.

And if memory becomes your framework, you will spend years watching wealth compound outside the walls of a prison you built yourself.

I'm still working on this one. Most days I get the question right. Some days I don't. The point is not to defeat the ghost. The point is to stop letting it sign the order tickets.

The market does not care where you first saw the stock.
It only cares what the business may be worth next.

Pull the anchor up.

That is how the ship moves.
