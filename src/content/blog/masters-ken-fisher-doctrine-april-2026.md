# The Masters: Ken Fisher Doctrine

## Why Fear Still Matters More Than Valuation — and Why the Market Usually Climbs the Wall of Worry

Ken Fisher remains one of the few market thinkers who can still irritate both permabulls and permabears at the same time.

He is too historically grounded to be a reckless optimist.

Too skeptical of popular narratives to be a conventional strategist.

Too willing to revise his own old frameworks to fit neatly into the "guru" category.

That is one reason investors still listen. Fisher Investments and its affiliates manage more than $387 billion as of March 31, 2026, and Ken Fisher remains the firm's Founder, Executive Chairman, and Co-Chief Investment Officer.

The real value in Fisher's thinking is not that he predicts the future with certainty.

It is that he provides a durable way to interpret markets when the crowd is trapped between fear and narrative.

His core message for 2026 is not "ignore risk." It is that investors **repeatedly misread risk because they obsess over what sounds scary instead of asking what is already widely known, widely feared, and therefore likely already in prices.**

That distinction is where Fisher often finds his edge.

Our view is straightforward: Fisher's framework is especially useful right now because U.S. investors are once again facing the classic late-cycle temptation to confuse discomfort with danger and high prices with imminent collapse. His doctrine argues that markets usually do not die from the fears everyone can name.

They more often stumble when optimism becomes effortless, skepticism disappears, and investors stop demanding a reason to doubt.

On that test, today's market still looks more conflicted than euphoric.

---

## 1. The Midterm Miracle: Why Gridlock Is Usually Bullish

One of Fisher's most famous market observations is the **"Midterm Miracle."**

He has argued for years that the strongest stretch of the U.S. presidential cycle tends to begin in the fourth quarter of the midterm election year and extend through much of the president's third year. In Fisher Investments' own midterm-election materials, Ken says the fourth quarters of midterm years are "overwhelmingly positive," and he has repeatedly tied that to reduced uncertainty and growing political gridlock after the election.

The intuition is elegant.

In the first half of a presidential term, investors worry about what a government with political momentum might still try to do. After the midterms, power is often more divided, large legislative swings become harder, and markets can breathe easier.

Fisher's interpretation is not that Washington becomes wise.

It is that Washington becomes slower — and markets usually like slower government better than ambitious government.

The broader historical record supports at least part of this pattern, even when one does not rely on Fisher's exact phrasing. Capital Group says the average one-year return following a U.S. midterm election since 1950 has been about 15.4%, nearly double comparable non-midterm periods. Business Insider recently summarized research noting that the S&P 500 has not declined in the 12 months following a midterm election since 1938.

That does not guarantee 2026–2027 will follow the script.

But it is enough to make the cycle worth respecting rather than dismissing.

That is the first major Fisher insight American investors should take seriously:

> **Political gridlock is not a bug in the U.S. system from a market perspective. It is often a feature.**

In a country where investors constantly fear policy shocks, the inability of either side to do too much too quickly can itself become a form of market support.

## 2. Bubble Talk Is Often a Contrary Indicator, Not a Warning Siren

Fisher's view on bubbles is uncomfortable for investors who want simple guardrails.

He has argued that markets are not typically in a true bubble when people are loudly and constantly calling them a bubble. In his October 2025 podcast, he discussed bubbles in the same breath as deficits and presidential cycles, emphasizing that widespread skepticism and ongoing fear are inconsistent with classic speculative mania.

In Fisher's framework, real bubbles are environments where fear largely disappears and investors see "only opportunity," not risk.

That distinction matters right now.

U.S. investors are surrounded by bubble language — AI bubble, mega-cap bubble, concentration bubble, passive bubble, fiscal bubble. Some of those concerns may eventually prove correct in part.

But Fisher's point is subtler:

> **When the entire investing class is already conditioned to fear excess, that fear itself can sustain a bull market longer than valuation purists expect.**

Markets often top not when caution is widespread, but when caution looks obsolete.

This is one of Fisher's most important contributions. He does not say optimism is always right. He says that the market's emotional backdrop matters as much as its multiple.

If investors are still arguing, still doubting, still waiting for the other shoe to drop, then one of the essential ingredients of a blowoff top may still be missing.

That does not eliminate downside.

It changes the burden of proof.

## 3. Valuation Is a Poor Timing Tool — And Fisher Knows It Because He Changed His Mind

One reason Fisher remains worth reading is that he has shown a willingness to revise his own earlier ideas.

He helped popularize the price-to-sales ratio decades ago, but over time he argued that once a metric becomes widely known and heavily used, its edge tends to erode. Even Wikipedia's summary of his work notes that in *The Only Three Questions That Count*, Fisher said PSR had become too widely used to retain the same forecasting power it once had.

More recently, Fisher Investments materials have emphasized that high valuations by themselves are poor short-to-medium-term market timing tools.

That matters enormously in the current U.S. market.

Investors still reach reflexively for valuation when they want to explain why something "must" fall. Fisher's doctrine is more frustrating and more useful:

- Expensive markets can keep rising
- Cheap markets can stay cheap
- Valuation alone rarely tells you much about the next one to five years

That does not mean valuation is irrelevant.

It means valuation is a weak stand-alone signal for timing a major turn.

For American investors, this is a liberating but dangerous idea. It is liberating because it reduces the false precision of calling tops based purely on multiples. It is dangerous because it can be misunderstood as permission to ignore price altogether.

Fisher's real point is not "anything goes."

It is that **investors should stop treating valuation as a magic countdown clock to a crash.** Markets need a catalyst, a shift in psychology, or a major reality break — not just a rich multiple.

## 4. "Never Yet Done": The Deepest Part of Fisher's Philosophy

The most intellectually interesting part of Fisher's doctrine is his idea that **capitalism's true engine is what has never yet been done.**

This is not just a growth slogan.

It is a way of seeing value creation as a process of adoption and behavioral transformation, not merely invention. Fisher has long argued that investors often overfocus on who created the technology and underfocus on who actually gets it embedded into real life.

That is why this framework travels so well into 2026.

In the current market, everyone talks about AI model builders, semiconductor designers, and infrastructure suppliers. Fisher's lens asks a harder and potentially more profitable question:

**Who will convert these technologies into ordinary human habits, workflows, and economic routines?**

That is where long-duration equity value often becomes much larger than the first-generation hardware excitement.

This is also why his philosophy remains so relevant to American investors. The U.S. equity market's greatest long-run winners are not always the first inventors. They are often the firms that take an emerging capability and turn it into ubiquitous usage, cultural habit, and scalable economics.

Fisher's doctrine pushes investors away from fetishizing novelty and toward analyzing adoption.

That is a much harder discipline — and a far more useful one.

## 5. What Fisher Would Likely Say About NVIDIA and the AI Trade

Fisher's framework does not say "sell the leader because it is obvious" or "buy forever because the story is great."

It says investors should own powerful structural winners until the demand story begins to outrun real adoption or hits a constraint that the market is not yet pricing. His 2025 outlook materials and broader commentary remain broadly constructive on global equities despite AI enthusiasm and rich valuations, which is consistent with the idea that strong narratives can continue as long as fear and skepticism still coexist.

Applied to NVIDIA, that means the critical question is not whether demand is strong today.

It plainly is.

The key question is whether AI deployment can continue broadening fast enough that demand for NVIDIA's output remains constrained by supply and adoption — not by saturation, regulation, or collapsing ROI.

A Fisher-style investor would probably care less about whether NVIDIA looks "expensive" in static terms and more about whether **the market's fear narrative has disappeared** and whether **end-market adoption is still widening rather than narrowing.**

That is a more sophisticated framework than simple momentum chasing and more realistic than valuation purism.

It also explains why many investors misread great growth stocks: they either marry the story or reject it reflexively. Fisher's doctrine asks you to follow the demand reality longer than feels comfortable, but not longer than the adoption reality lasts.

## 6. "Think Global, Invest Global": The Anti–Home Bias Lesson Matters Even More Now

One of Fisher's most durable teachings is that investors should think globally first.

This is especially provocative for Americans because U.S. investors often assume their home market is not just the best market, but the only market that matters. Fisher has consistently argued that home bias is a risk, not a virtue, and that investors should understand their own country in the context of the world rather than as the world itself.

Fisher Investments' own corporate background emphasizes a global capital-markets view and a philosophy rooted in free capital markets broadly, not just domestic allocation.

This lesson is not anti-American.

In fact, it may be the most intelligent form of American investing. The U.S. remains the dominant equity market, but **dominance can encourage intellectual laziness.** A Fisher-style investor uses the U.S. as the core not because of habit, but because it continues to offer many of the world's best businesses.

That is a subtle but important difference.

It means one should be pro-America without being analytically provincial.

For 2026, this is especially relevant because many investors are once again tempted to let one narrative — AI-led U.S. mega-cap leadership — stand in for all market analysis. Fisher's global framework is a useful antidote.

It does not require bearishness on America.

It requires awareness that concentration, leadership, and opportunity are different things. The U.S. can remain dominant while other markets still generate meaningful opportunity at the margin.

---

## 7. The Deepest Lesson: Know Thyself Before You Size Risk

Perhaps Fisher's most underrated contribution is not a market call at all.

It is his insistence that **investing forces self-knowledge.**

Books matter, data matter, history matters — but eventually the market makes investors confront their own temperament. That is not a soft point. It is a portfolio point.

The investor who does not understand their own tolerance for drawdowns, volatility, envy, boredom, and regret will eventually pay for that ignorance in real money.

This is where Fisher is much more psychologically sophisticated than many "rules-based" commentators. He understands that the market is not just a test of theories.

It is a test of identity.

That is why the right investor response to his doctrine is not to imitate his conclusions mechanically. It is to adopt his habit of asking:

- What are markets saying?
- What is already widely believed?
- What fear still remains?
- Are your own reactions helping or hurting you?

---

## 8. Investment Conclusion

The strongest version of the Ken Fisher doctrine for U.S. investors in 2026 is this:

> **Do not confuse fear with fragility.**
> **Do not confuse high multiples with imminent collapse.**
> **Do not confuse invention with investability.**
> **Do not confuse your home market with the whole world.**
> **And do not confuse your portfolio with your ego.**

Fisher's framework remains powerful because it is not built on certainty.

It is built on historical asymmetry, investor psychology, and the persistent tendency of markets to disappoint the consensus at the wrong time.

The U.S. midterm cycle may again provide a supportive backdrop. Bubble fears may still be more evidence of skepticism than mania. Valuation may remain a bad timing tool. And the biggest winners of the next cycle may still be the companies that turn technological possibility into ordinary economic behavior.

Our own view is constructive, but disciplined.

Fisher is not giving investors permission to become euphoric. He is telling them that the market usually rises not when everything is safe, but when **fear remains loud and reality remains better than expected.**

For American investors, that is the right challenge right now:

Stay bullish enough to participate.

Skeptical enough to avoid fantasy.

Flexible enough to recognize that the market's favorite surprise is still the one most people talk themselves out of owning.

---

## Final Line

Ken Fisher's real doctrine is not optimism.

It is this: **markets usually reward the investor who can think historically while everyone else is reacting emotionally.**
