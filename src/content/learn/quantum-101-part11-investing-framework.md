# Quantum Computing 101 — Part 11

## The Quantum Investing Framework: How to Separate Real Platforms from Expensive Science Projects

> **About this series.** This is an educational resource for readers learning how to *evaluate* quantum-computing companies. It is not investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell any security. The frameworks below describe the questions and metrics specialists use; they are tools for reading the news, not a model portfolio. Companies are named only as examples of where each layer sits.
>
> **Disclosure:** The author holds a position in IonQ (IONQ), one of the companies referenced below. This is disclosed in the interest of transparency and does not constitute a recommendation.

So far we have covered the major quantum-computing technologies and applications: superconducting (fast chip), trapped ion (precise atom), neutral atom (large grid of atoms), photonic (networkable light), silicon spin (semiconductor-like), topological (error-resistant, high-risk), quantum annealing (optimization-specialized), the software/cloud "operating system" layer, post-quantum cryptography, and applications across drugs, materials, finance, logistics, energy, defense, and security.

This part ties everything together into a framework.

In one middle-school sentence: **Quantum investing is not a game of picking "the coolest technology." It is a game of judging who can survive long enough to actually solve customer problems and make money.**

---

## 1. Quantum Computing Is Not Yet a "Winner-Take-All" Market

The first idea to discard is **"which approach is the final winner?"** Someday winners and losers will separate, but right now it is too early. Superconducting, trapped ion, neutral atom, photonic, silicon spin, topological, and annealing each have different strengths and weaknesses. Today's quantum market resembles 1950s–60s computing more than the smartphone market. The standard form factor is not yet set, and what customers will pay — and how much — is still being validated.

So investors should hold a **portfolio map** rather than "one right answer":
- Some approaches are strong on error resistance.
- Some are strong on manufacturing scalability.
- Some are strong on cloud access.
- Some fit government budgets well.
- Some may earn money first in optimization.

Quantum computing is still a technology race, a capital race, and a patience race all at once.

---

## 2. Divide Quantum Investing into Four Layers

To see quantum investing properly, do not line companies up in a single row. Comparing "IBM vs IonQ vs Rigetti vs D-Wave" is wrong because they sit in different layers.

**Layer 1 — Hardware.** Companies building actual quantum computers: IBM, Google/Alphabet, IonQ, Quantinuum, Rigetti, D-Wave, Atom Computing, PsiQuantum, QuEra, Pasqal, Diraq, Quantum Motion, Microsoft. Biggest potential upside, biggest technology risk.

**Layer 2 — Cloud and Software.** Layers that let customers actually use quantum computers: IBM Qiskit, Microsoft Azure Quantum, Amazon Braket, Google Cirq, Xanadu PennyLane, Classiq, QC Ware. This layer grows more important as hardware approaches diverge — because customers want *problems solved*, not a specific qubit type.

**Layer 3 — Security and Infrastructure.** Changing cryptography and security infrastructure for the quantum era: PQC, certificate management, key management, HSM, Zero Trust, cloud and network security. Companies: Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Cloudflare, Palo Alto Networks, Zscaler, CrowdStrike, IBM Security, Thales, Entrust, DigiCert. This layer can see demand *before* quantum computers are finished — NIST released its first three PQC standards in 2024, and governments and enterprises are already in the inventory-and-transition phase.

**Layer 4 — Applications.** Customer problems where quantum could make money: drugs, materials and batteries, chemistry, financial risk and portfolios, logistics and supply chains, energy and grid, defense and aerospace, security. The biggest market long-term comes from this layer — but it may open last.

---

## 3. The U.S. Government Is Building a Policy Floor

In May 2026, the U.S. Department of Commerce announced letters of intent for a total of **$2.013 billion** in proposed incentives to 9 quantum companies under the CHIPS and Science Act — a package supporting two domestic quantum foundries and seven quantum-computing companies to accelerate utility-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computers.

This is not just subsidy news. It signals that the U.S. government views quantum computing as a **national strategic technology** — much as semiconductors were re-rated as a strategic industry after the CHIPS Act, quantum is increasingly classified as national-security infrastructure.

When a policy floor forms, three things change: capital-raising becomes easier, government customers and defense projects grow, and technology companies' survival time lengthens. That said, **policy support is not investment success.** Government support raises industry survival odds but does not remove individual-stock valuation risk.

---

## 4. The Market Is Large, but the Timeline Is Long

McKinsey's Quantum Technology Monitor 2025 projected quantum-computing revenue could grow from about **$4 billion in 2024** to as much as **$72 billion by 2035**; including all quantum technology (computing, communication, sensing), it suggested a potential market of up to **$97 billion by 2035**.

These numbers are attractive — but caution is needed. A large 2035 market does not mean 2026 stocks are cheap. Quantum companies still have small revenue, large losses, and many sit between technical validation and commercialization. So quantum investing must be examined more strictly than ordinary growth stocks.

The question is not **"is this company in a big market?"** but **"can this company survive until that market opens?"**

---

## 5. Ten Questions for Evaluating a Hardware Company

When evaluating quantum hardware, do not look only at "qubit count" — it matters, but it is also the easiest marketing number.

**1. How is qubit quality?** Good qubits beat many qubits: single-qubit fidelity, two-qubit fidelity, readout fidelity, coherence time, error rate.

**2. Is there an error-correction roadmap?** Logical-qubit demonstration, surface code or another error-correction code, a fault-tolerant roadmap, whether error rate is below threshold.

**3. Is real circuit depth growing?** IBM targets up to 360 qubits and 7,500-gate circuits on Nighthawk-based systems in 2026, expanding to 10,000 gates in 2027 and 15,000 in 2028. Such metrics are closer to real computing power than raw qubit count.

**4. Is there manufacturing scalability?** Foundry partnership, wafer-scale manufacturing, photonic integration, cryogenic control, supply chain.

**5. Are customers actually paying?** Announcements are plentiful; contracts and revenue matter. IonQ raised its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to **$260–$270 million** (Q2: $65–$68 million) — a rare scale of commercial momentum among pure-play quantum companies (see disclosure above).

**6. Are there government projects?** DARPA, DOE, Department of Commerce, and defense/intelligence projects matter for credibility and funding.

**7. Is there enough cash?** Quantum companies can run losses for a long time; without cash, financing becomes a bigger problem than the technology roadmap.

**8. Is the approach's competitiveness clear?** Does the company clearly explain the strengths and weaknesses of its modality?

**9. Is there software and cloud access?** Customers do not want to touch hardware directly; cloud and SDKs matter.

**10. Does valuation match the technology stage?** Even the best technology can be a bad investment if too expensive.

---

## 6. Divide Quantum Companies into Three Groups

**Group 1 — Diversified Platform Companies:** IBM, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Honeywell. Not pure-plays — even if quantum succeeds, the stock impact is limited. The advantage is stability. This group holds a **long-term quantum option inside a large platform.** Suited to investors who want lower volatility and want to view quantum alongside AI, cloud, enterprise, and semiconductors.

**Group 2 — Public Quantum Pure-Plays:** IonQ, Rigetti, D-Wave, Quantinuum, Quantum Computing Inc., and others. More direct exposure — bigger upside, bigger volatility. Quantinuum recently entered the public market via a traditional IPO: its shares opened at $68 (above the $60 IPO price), putting the Honeywell-backed firm at roughly a **$17.63 billion valuation** at the open; at the same time, its 2025 revenue was about $30.9 million with a net loss of about $192.6 million — still an early, high-loss stage. This captures the whole pure-play picture: **expectations are large, but current financials are early.**

**Group 3 — Private and Pre-IPO Quantum Companies:** PsiQuantum, Atom Computing, QuEra, Pasqal, Diraq, Quantum Motion, Xanadu, Classiq. Hard for retail to invest in directly, but they shape the industry's direction, and can influence public markets via IPO, SPAC, strategic acquisition, cloud partnership, or government support.

## 7. Investment Character by Technology

| Approach | Representative firms | Investment character | Core risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Superconducting | IBM, Google, Rigetti | Fast gates, big-company ecosystem | Cryogenics, errors, wiring |
| Trapped ion | IonQ, Quantinuum | Accuracy, connectivity, high pure-play exposure | Speed, modular scaling |
| Neutral atom | Atom, QuEra, Pasqal | Large qubit arrays | Error-correction proof |
| Photonic | PsiQuantum, Xanadu | Networking, manufacturing scalability | Photon loss, interaction |
| Silicon spin | Intel, Diraq, Quantum Motion | Semiconductor-process scalability | Control, connection, cryogenics |
| Topological | Microsoft | Possibility of error-resistant qubits | Least proven |
| Annealing | D-Wave | Optimization commercialization | Limited universality |

The key point: there is no "best approach" yet. Each solves different problems.

## 8. The Biggest Valuation Traps in Quantum Investing

**Trap 1 — Investing on qubit count alone.** A million bad qubits can be worth less than 1,000 error-correctable ones.

**Trap 2 — Treating government funding like revenue.** Funding is good, but it does not guarantee recurring revenue or profitability.

**Trap 3 — Overrating partnerships.** "Collaborating" with a big company does not guarantee large revenue; look at contract size, term, and obligations.

**Trap 4 — Treating every quantum company like NVIDIA.** AI chips and quantum computing differ; NVIDIA already has enormous real demand and profit, which most quantum companies do not.

**Trap 5 — Mistaking announcements for commercialization.** Papers, demos, and benchmarks matter, but differ from customers paying and using repeatedly.

**Trap 6 — Ignoring dilution risk.** Quantum companies keep needing capital; issuing stock while loss-making dilutes existing shareholders.

**Trap 7 — Confusing "right long-term" with "right at this price."** An industry can grow while a specific overvalued stock delivers poor returns.

## 9. Key Financial Metrics for Quantum Companies

Traditional P/E is hard to apply since most run losses. Instead watch:

**(1)** revenue growth, **(2)** backlog / remaining performance obligations (contracted future revenue), **(3)** bookings (new demand), **(4)** gross margin (real economics), **(5)** cash balance (years of runway), **(6)** operating cash burn (quarterly), **(7)** dilution (share-count growth), **(8)** government funding (non-dilutive vs equity-stake conditions), **(9)** customer concentration, and **(10)** technical-milestone credibility (is the roadmap being met).

## 10. Portfolio Approaches

Quantum investing is a high-risk, long-term theme, so portfolio construction matters. (Illustrative groupings, not recommendations.)

**Conservative** — large platforms (IBM, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Honeywell): lower volatility, non-quantum business base; but low stock leverage to quantum success.

**Balanced** — large platforms plus a small amount of pure-plays (e.g., some of IonQ / Rigetti / D-Wave / Quantinuum): balances stability and upside; requires managing pure-play volatility.

**Aggressive** — pure-play-centric (IonQ, Rigetti, D-Wave, Quantinuum, other public quantum names): large upside if the industry succeeds; high volatility, dilution, technology-failure, and valuation risk.

**Barbell** — holding both extremes: stable platforms (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon) on one side, high-risk pure-plays (IonQ, Rigetti, D-Wave) on the other — capturing pure-play upside while keeping platform stability.

## 11. The Timeline Every Investor Must Hold

Quantum computing is risky viewed quarterly, interesting viewed over a decade.

**2026–2028: Validation and policy support.** Watch government funding, roadmap delivery, revenue growth, customer pilots, cloud usage, PQC transition, logical-qubit experiments.

**2028–2032: Early commercialization.** Watch quantum advantage on specific industrial problems, error-correction progress, hardware consolidation, IPOs and M&A, integration into large cloud platforms.

**After 2032: The truly large market.** Watch fault-tolerant systems, large-scale applications in drugs/materials/finance/energy, quantum networks, national infrastructure, market reshaping.

The timeline shows: **quantum investing is not fast money; it is a long option.**

---

## 12. A Quantum Investor's Checklist

Before investing, it helps to ask:

**(1)** Which modality does this company build?
**(2)** What are its strengths and weaknesses?
**(3)** Are error rate and circuit depth improving, not just qubit count?
**(4)** Are customers actually paying?
**(5)** Are there government projects?
**(6)** How many years of cash remain?
**(7)** How severe is dilution?
**(8)** Is there software and cloud access?
**(9)** Is there another revenue source if the modality fails?
**(10)** Does the current valuation already price in too much future success?
**(11)** What percentage of my portfolio can I tolerate here?
**(12)** Can I logically withstand a 50% drop?

The last question matters most. For quantum pure-plays, a 50% drawdown is not unusual. If you cannot withstand that volatility, large platforms or an ETF-style approach may suit better than pure-plays.

---

## 13. The Final Frame for U.S. Investors

Quantum computing is not a market where revenue and profit explode immediately like AI. But it is one of the most important long-term computing-paradigm candidates after AI. The U.S. government is reclassifying quantum as a national strategic technology, large platforms are building the hardware/software/cloud/security layers, and pure-plays keep entering public markets. The 2026 Commerce Department $2.013 billion incentives and Quantinuum's traditional IPO signal that quantum is no longer only a lab theme.

Still, investors must stay cold-eyed: quantum is early, the technology is hard, commercialization is slow, valuations overheat easily, and pure-plays are volatile. So the investing frame should be:

> **Quantum computing is not a single-stock prediction game. It is a structural investing game that weighs technology approach, capital strength, government support, customer revenue, software ecosystem, and timeline together.**

## Final Line

> **Quantum investing is not about finding the company with the loudest roadmap. It is about finding the companies that can survive long enough, execute clearly enough, and own enough of the stack when quantum moves from scientific promise to economic utility.**

To compress it:

> **The core of quantum investing is not "which technology is coolest" but "who can survive long enough to solve real problems, generate customer revenue, and become part of national strategic infrastructure."**
